# Expert References: literature-review

## Literature anchors

1. **Webster, J. & Watson, R. T. (2002). "Analyzing the Past to Prepare for the Future: Writing a Literature Review." *MIS Quarterly*, 26(2), xiii–xxiii.**
   - What we took from it: The author-centric vs. concept-centric distinction and the argument that a real review is organized around concepts, not papers. Also their concept matrix as a tool for moving from corpus to draft.
   - How it shaped this skill: This is the single most cited move in the Expert Knowledge section. The "if your draft can be reordered without changing meaning, it isn't a review" framing comes directly from their critique. The Workflow's diagnosis-before-drafting step is essentially their concept-matrix construction in lightweight form.

2. **Boote, D. N. & Beile, P. (2005). "Scholars Before Researchers: On the Centrality of the Dissertation Literature Review in Research Preparation." *Educational Researcher*, 34(6), 3–15.**
   - What we took from it: Their five-criteria framework for what a quality literature review demonstrates — coverage, synthesis, methodology critique, significance, and rhetoric. Especially their argument that the review demonstrates the candidate's *understanding*, not merely their reading.
   - How it shaped this skill: The "understanding, not coverage" line in Expert Knowledge is theirs. The emphasis on critical voice as judicious assessment (not destructive critique) and the framing of gap identification as the review's payoff both trace to their criteria.

3. **Galvan, J. L. & Galvan, M. C. (2017). *Writing Literature Reviews: A Guide for Students of the Social and Behavioral Sciences* (7th ed.). Routledge.**
   - What we took from it: A concrete, student-facing version of the matrix method — the practical table-of-papers-by-attributes that lets a student see themes emerge from columns rather than try to invent themes from memory.
   - How it shaped this skill: Workflow step 3 (diagnose the corpus) is the matrix method compressed. Their student-tone register also influenced the skill's voice — opinionated, practical, like a TA explaining over coffee rather than a textbook chapter.

4. **Hart, C. (2018). *Doing a Literature Review: Releasing the Research Imagination* (2nd ed.). SAGE.**
   - What we took from it: The taxonomy of organizational schemes (thematic, chronological, methodological, theoretical) and the argument for choosing one defensible top-level structure rather than mixing schemes incoherently. Also his treatment of the review as an *argument*, not a survey.
   - How it shaped this skill: The three-scheme typology in Expert Knowledge is Hart's, slightly compressed. The recurring "review is an argument about a body of work" framing is also his.

5. **Kitchenham, B. & Charters, S. (2007). *Guidelines for Performing Systematic Literature Reviews in Software Engineering*. EBSE Technical Report EBSE-2007-01.**
   - What we took from it: The procedural distinction between narrative and systematic reviews, including pre-registered protocols, explicit inclusion/exclusion criteria, and PRISMA-style filtering documentation.
   - How it shaped this skill: The Workflow's first step explicitly surfaces narrative vs. systematic vs. chapter as a branch, because failing to ask risks producing the wrong deliverable. The systematic-review scenario in scenarios.jsonl uses Kitchenham conventions.

## Workflow anchors

1. **Boote & Beile (2005), "Five Criteria for a Quality Literature Review" (the rubric they propose in the same *Educational Researcher* article).**
   - Workflow we copied:
     - Treat the review as an argument requiring a thesis-like claim, not a comprehensive enumeration.
     - Demand synthesis across sources (multiple citations per claim) rather than per-paper paragraphs.
     - Require critical assessment of methodology, not just findings.
     - Require the review to motivate the candidate's own work (gap-as-payoff).
   - What we adapted: We collapsed their five criteria into the four-component skill structure (Expert Knowledge / Workflow / Output Template / Examples) the methodology mandates. We also softened "rhetoric" (their fifth criterion) into the Output Template's voice and structure rather than treating it as a separate evaluative dimension. Their criteria assume a dissertation-length review; we adapted the skill to work for shorter chapter sections and workshop-paper-length reviews as well.

2. **The University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill Writing Center's Literature Reviews handout (writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/literature-reviews/).**
   - Workflow we copied:
     - Their explicit advice that lit-review drafting begins with finding the *organizing principle* (theme, debate, method, or chronology) rather than starting with the first paper.
     - Their concrete revision prompts (e.g., "Could a reader rearrange your paragraphs without changing the argument?") which we adapted into the diagnostic check in Expert Knowledge.
     - Their distinction between summary and synthesis, illustrated with paired example sentences.
   - What we adapted: The handout addresses an undergraduate-through-early-graduate audience; we tightened the register for master's/PhD students, assumed more disciplinary literacy, and folded the synthesis-vs-summary example directly into the Expert Knowledge prose rather than treating it as a standalone exercise.
